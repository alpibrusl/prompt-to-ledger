# Confidence, Not Certainty

"We have eleven months of runway." Said plainly, it sounds like a fact with no give in it. It rarely is one. The eleven is a best guess, built on assumptions that could each turn out slightly different, and the honest version of that sentence has a range attached to it whether or not anyone says the range out loud.

## The number is a guess, precisely stated

A **point estimate** is the single best-guess number a forecast produces — the eleven months, the tripled revenue, the projected margin. It is real, it is the right answer to give when someone asks "what's your best guess," and it is also, always, a guess: computed from the particular set of assumptions you happened to choose, which would come out slightly different with slightly different, equally defensible assumptions.

**Scenario analysis** is building more than one version of the forecast — typically a base case, a better case, and a worse case — each with its own internally consistent set of assumptions, rather than presenting one number as if it were the only possible outcome. "Eleven months of runway" said alone hides a question that changes everything about what to do with it: is the honest range nine to thirteen months, or is it nine months at one end and never running out at the other? The first is a result you can plan around with real confidence. The second means the difference between urgent and comfortable is a single assumption, sitting quietly underneath a headline number that sounded completely settled.

## Building a range that means something

The temptation is to build the range by feel — knock twenty percent off for the worst case, add twenty percent for the best. Resist it. A range built that way is decoration, not information; it looks like rigor and carries none of it.

A real range comes from doing what Chapter 8 already taught: identify the one or two load-bearing assumptions, and vary *those* specifically across defensible values, not every input by an arbitrary percentage. Ledgerly's base case uses the four-percent monthly growth rate Chapter 8 grounded in the company's own history. A worse case might use two percent — still positive, still a real scenario, not a manufactured disaster. A better case might use six percent — ambitious, and still nowhere near the ten percent that started this whole story. Run the same model, the one built with real parameters back in Chapter 5, against each growth rate, and three honestly different answers come out the other end: in the worse case the cash runs out in the ninth month; in the base case, the eleventh; and in the better case it never runs out at all — revenue overtakes costs early in the second year, with the balance bottoming out around €34,000 on the way there.

That's a range worth presenting because every number in it came from an assumption someone would be willing to defend out loud, not from an arbitrary buffer added to make the presentation look appropriately cautious.

## What the range is telling you

Width is the part worth reading, more than the point estimate itself. A narrow range — worst and best cases close together — means the forecast doesn't depend much on the assumptions that are genuinely uncertain, and the base case is a reasonable thing to plan around directly. A wide range means the opposite: the honest answer is "somewhere between comfortable and urgent," even though the single headline number reports one specific point inside that range with exactly the same confident formatting either way.

Ledgerly's range — from nine months to not running out at all, depending almost entirely on how fast growth actually lands — is a genuinely useful thing to know walking into a fundraising conversation. It says: start raising now because the base case leaves eleven months and a round takes six of them; and don't let the better case relax you because its low point is about one month of the current burn, thin enough that a single slow-paying quarter would erase it. A single point estimate of eleven months doesn't say any of that. It just says eleven, and lets you infer the urgency wrong in either direction.

## The decision this changes

Two forecasts can carry the identical headline number and mean entirely different things once the range is visible. "Eleven months, range nine to thirteen" is a result you can plan a fundraise timeline around with real confidence. "Eleven months, range nine to never running out" is a result where the honest position is closer to *start now and move fast* than to *we have a comfortable runway* — and no amount of repeating the point estimate more confidently changes which of those two situations you're actually in.

The practical rule: before acting on a forecast, ask whether the range is narrow enough, relative to the decision on the table, to actually tell your options apart. If the range spans the line between "comfortable" and "urgent," you do not yet have enough certainty to relax, regardless of which side of that line the point estimate happens to sit on. That is not a failure of the model. It is the model correctly reporting that the outcome genuinely depends on things that haven't happened yet.

## What to ask for

> "Don't just give me the number — give me a base case, a worse case, and a better case, each built from assumptions you can name and defend."

The default request. A forecast with no range attached is a headline, not yet something you can plan around.

> "How wide is this range, relative to the decision I'm actually making? Does the worse case change what I should do differently, starting now?"

The single most decision-relevant question this chapter has.

> "Which assumptions actually moved between these three scenarios, and are all three still ones you'd defend if asked?"

Confirms the range came from Chapter 8's real sensitivity work, not from an arbitrary buffer dressed up as scenario planning.
