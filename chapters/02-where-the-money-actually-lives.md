# Where the Money Actually Lives

Ledgerly's dashboard says the business made a profit last month. Its bank balance is lower than it was a month ago. Both of these are true at the same time, and neither one is a mistake.

This chapter is about that gap: why "profitable" and "cash went up" are different claims, and the three places a business's financial story actually gets written down.

## Two ways of counting the same thing

**Cash accounting** counts a transaction when money actually moves — a sale is recorded when the customer pays, a bill is recorded when it's settled. It is exactly how a personal bank account works, and it is easy to reason about for exactly that reason: the balance you see is the balance you have.

**Accrual accounting** counts a transaction when it's earned or incurred, regardless of when cash moves. A sale is recorded the moment you deliver what was promised, whether the invoice is paid today or in sixty days. A cost is recorded when you become obligated to pay it, not when the payment clears.

Many established businesses use accrual accounting, especially once the timing gap between earning revenue and collecting cash becomes material — some smaller businesses legitimately run on cash accounting instead, depending on size and jurisdiction, but accrual is the default once a business is past its earliest days, for a specific reason: it matches revenue to the period it was actually earned in, which is what makes one month comparable to the next. Cash accounting can make a business look wildly profitable the month a big invoice finally gets paid, and wildly unprofitable the surrounding months when the work was actually done. Accrual accounting spreads the story out to where the story actually happened.

The cost of that accuracy is the gap this chapter is about: an accrual-basis business can report a profit in a month when cash in the bank went down, and report a loss in a month when cash went up. Neither number is lying. They are answering different questions.

## The three statements

A business's financial story is written down in three places, and each one answers a genuinely different question.

The **income statement** — also called a P&L, short for profit and loss — answers: *did this period's activity make money?* Revenue earned, minus the costs of earning it, over a period of time. This is where "profit" and "loss" live, and it is built on accrual accounting: a sale counts here the moment it's earned, whether or not the cash has arrived yet.

The **balance sheet** answers a different question: *what does the business own, and what does it owe, right now?* Not over a period — at a single instant, like a photograph rather than a video. What it owns (cash, money customers owe you, equipment) minus what it owes (money you owe suppliers, loans) is the business's accounting equity — its book value, on paper, at that moment. That is a real number and a useful one, but it is not the same claim as what an investor or a buyer would actually pay for the business; book value is what accounting leaves over, not a market price.

The **cash flow statement** answers the question the other two can't: *how much actual cash moved, and where did it go?* Under the method most businesses use, it starts from the accrual-basis profit on the income statement and adjusts it back toward cash for the operating side of the business — subtracting the sale that was earned but not yet paid (profit already counted it, but the cash isn't in the bank yet), subtracting the bill that was paid but not yet earned as an expense (the cash is already out the door, but profit hasn't counted it yet either) — and then adds in the cash that moved for reasons that have nothing to do with day-to-day operations: money spent buying equipment, money raised from a loan or an investor. All three pieces together — operating, investing, financing — are what explain the real change in the bank balance; the operating adjustment alone only tells part of the story.

Ledgerly's confusing month makes sense once you see it through all three. The income statement said profit, because revenue earned that month (including work already delivered to customers who hadn't paid yet) exceeded costs incurred. The cash flow statement told the truer story for what actually happened to the bank balance: two large customers were slow to pay, so the cash side of that same revenue hadn't landed yet, while payroll — a real cash cost — went out on schedule regardless.

## Why this distinction is the one that costs people money

Of the mistakes this book exists to catch, believing an income statement tells you whether you can make payroll next month is one of the most common and most avoidable. The income statement can report a healthy profit while the bank account empties, if enough revenue is sitting uncollected and enough real cash is going out the door on schedule. A business can survive a stretch of accounting losses. What it cannot survive indefinitely is being unable to meet its obligations when the cash to cover them isn't there — profitability and solvency are not the same event, and it's the second one that ends a business on the spot.

This is not a reason to distrust the income statement — it answers a real question, and it's the right one for understanding whether the underlying business model works. It's a reason to never let it stand in alone for the question that actually threatens a business day to day, which is what Chapter 9 is entirely about.

## What to ask for

> "Is this number from the income statement, the balance sheet, or the cash flow statement — and which one am I actually trying to answer a question with?"

The single most useful habit in this chapter, asked before trusting any number that sounds like "the business made X."

> "Show me this month on an accrual basis and on a cash basis, side by side. Where do they disagree, and why?"

If the two stories match closely, that's real information. If they don't, the size of the gap tells you how much revenue is sitting uncollected — which is worth knowing before it becomes a surprise.

> "What does the balance sheet say we own and owe right now, not just what did we earn this month?"

The photograph, not the video — and the one of the three statements people ask for least, despite it being where "can we actually afford this" ultimately gets answered.
