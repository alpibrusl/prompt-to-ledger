# A Chart Is a Claim

The same twelve months of Ledgerly's revenue can be drawn a dozen honest ways and look like a dozen different stories. Nothing about a chart being clean, professional, and correctly labelled tells you whether the particular way it was drawn happens to be the one that flatters the point someone wanted to make.

## Every choice in a chart is an editorial one

A chart is not a neutral photograph of the numbers behind it. Someone — or, now, an agent acting on a request — decided where the axis starts, which months to include, whether to show revenue alone or revenue against costs. None of those choices are forced by the data itself; each one is a decision, and each decision can tell the same underlying numbers as a dramatic story or a modest one without a single value being altered.

**Where the axis starts.** A revenue line climbing gently from €18,000 to €22,000 a month looks like a modest change on an axis running from €0 to €50,000, and looks like a dramatic one on an axis running from €17,000 to €23,000. Neither is automatically the lie — a chart tracking a metric that's meaningfully different at every point along a narrow range sometimes genuinely needs a tighter axis to be readable at all. The question worth asking isn't "does it start at zero," it's "would the story change if it did" — and if the honest answer is yes, that's worth knowing before you repeat the story in a deck.

**Which months are shown.** A chart covering Ledgerly's three best months tells a real, accurate, entirely misleading story about the year. The fix is not complicated: ask what the same chart looks like extended back through the slower months, and treat a chart that was never shown that way as incomplete rather than dishonest — the distinction rarely matters to the decision either way.

**What got left off entirely.** An **aggregation** is a single summary value standing in for many underlying ones — total revenue for the year standing in for twelve months that moved very differently from each other, an average monthly growth rate standing in for a curve that was actually flat for months and then jumped once. A revenue chart with no cost line next to it is this same problem wearing a different shape: it answers "did revenue grow" without answering the question that actually determines whether growing revenue was good news, which is whether the cost of generating it grew just as fast.

## One chart, one point

There is a failure mode that has nothing to do with dishonesty and does almost as much damage: a chart genuinely trying to be thorough, carrying revenue, costs, headcount, and burn on the same crowded axis, that nobody can actually read in the time a board meeting allows for it. If understanding what it claims takes ten minutes of someone talking you through it, it has already failed at the one thing a chart is for.

This is not only a clarity problem. It's the same trust problem the rest of this chapter is about, arriving from a different direction. The test below — would the story hold up drawn the plain, obvious way — needs a single identifiable story to test in the first place. Four metrics crammed onto one chart have no one story; they have as many as there are lines, and no one has the patience to audit each of them, so the whole thing gets nodded past instead of actually read.

## The test that doesn't require a finance background

You do not need training in charting to catch most of this. One question does most of the work: **would this chart tell a meaningfully different story if drawn the obvious alternative way** — axis starting at zero, the full year instead of the best quarter, costs shown alongside revenue instead of revenue alone?

If the story holds up across those alternatives, you're very likely looking at an honest representation, whichever specific version you were handed. If the story changes the moment you ask for one of those alternatives, that's the finding — not proof of dishonesty, since these choices get made constantly without any intent to mislead, but a real signal that the specific version in front of you was the flattering one, deliberately or not.

## What to ask for

> "Show me this same chart with the axis starting at zero, and with the full year instead of just this window. Does the story still hold?"

The direct version of the test above — cheap to ask for, and it either confirms the chart or reveals which choices were doing the work.

> "Show me costs on the same chart as revenue, not revenue alone."

Catches the aggregation problem specifically — growing revenue that's actually being outpaced by growing costs, hidden by a chart that only ever shows one side of the story.

> "Could someone unfamiliar with this business understand what this chart is claiming within about ten seconds, without you explaining it? If not, simplify it — fewer metrics, one clear comparison, or split it into more than one chart."

The complexity test. A chart that needs a guided tour has failed at its one job regardless of how accurate every number on it is.
