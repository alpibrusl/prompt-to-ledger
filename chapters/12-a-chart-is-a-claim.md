# A Chart Is a Claim

Ledgerly's trailing year is one file holding twelve numbers. Drawn one way it shows a company growing steadily. Drawn another it shows a company about to run out of money. Drawn a third it shows a takeoff worth tripling a forecast on. All three are accurate, none has been touched, and only one of them was in the deck.

Clean, professional and correctly labelled says nothing about which of the available true charts you were handed.

## Every choice in a chart is an editorial one

A chart is an argument about numbers, not a photograph of them. Someone — or, now, an agent acting on a request — decided where the axis starts, which months to include, whether to show revenue alone or revenue against costs. None of those choices are forced by the data itself; each one is a decision, and each decision can tell the same underlying numbers as a dramatic story or a modest one without a single value being altered.

**Where the axis starts.** A revenue line climbing gently from €18,000 to €22,000 a month looks like a modest change on an axis running from €0 to €50,000, and looks like a dramatic one on an axis running from €17,000 to €23,000. Neither is automatically the lie — a chart tracking a metric that's meaningfully different at every point along a narrow range sometimes genuinely needs a tighter axis to be readable at all. "Does it start at zero" is the wrong question. "Would the story change if it did" is the right one, and where the answer is yes, you want to know that before the chart goes in front of anyone.

**Which months are shown.** Ledgerly's revenue grew about three percent a month for ten months and then about ten percent for two. Plot the full year and the eye reports a steady climb. Plot the last quarter alone and it reports a company that has just found its market — which is the reading "revenue triples" was built on. Both charts are the same file.

The window does the same work on the cash side, and there it decides something. Project Ledgerly's cash at the growth rate the year actually shows and cut the chart at month twelve: the line falls the whole way and is still falling when the page ends. Draw thirty-six months instead and the same line bottoms out at €34,787 in month eighteen and climbs back. One chart says the company is dying. The other says it will be tight in a year and a half. Neither invented a number. The fix is not complicated: ask what the same chart looks like extended back through the slower months, and treat a chart that was never shown that way as incomplete rather than dishonest — the distinction rarely matters to the decision either way.

**What got left off entirely.** An **aggregation** is a single summary value standing in for many underlying ones — total revenue for the year standing in for twelve months that moved very differently from each other, an average monthly growth rate standing in for a curve that was actually flat for months and then jumped once. A revenue chart with no cost line next to it is this same problem wearing a different shape: it answers "did revenue grow" without answering the question that actually determines whether growing revenue was good news, which is whether the cost of generating it grew just as fast.

## One chart, one point

There is a failure mode that has nothing to do with dishonesty and does almost as much damage: a chart genuinely trying to be thorough, carrying revenue, costs, headcount, and burn on the same crowded axis, that nobody can actually read in the time a board meeting allows for it. If understanding what it claims takes ten minutes of someone talking you through it, it has already failed at the one thing a chart is for.

That is not only a clarity problem; it is the trust problem arriving from a different direction. The test below asks whether a story survives being drawn plainly, and a chart carrying four metrics has no single story to put to it. Four metrics crammed onto one chart have no one story; they have as many as there are lines, and no one has the patience to audit each of them, so the whole thing gets nodded past instead of actually read.

## The test that doesn't require a finance background

You do not need training in charting to catch most of this. One question does most of the work: **would this chart tell a meaningfully different story if drawn the obvious alternative way** — axis starting at zero, the full year instead of the best quarter, costs shown alongside revenue instead of revenue alone?

A story that survives all three is very probably honest, whichever version reached you. A story that collapses under one of them is the finding — not evidence that anyone lied, since these choices get made all day without intent, but evidence that the version you were given was the flattering one.

Ledgerly's revenue chart fails the third. Revenue climbed every month of the trailing year, from €12,175 to €20,000, and a chart of that line alone is a company doing well. Costs sat near €35,000 throughout. Put both lines on one axis and the gap never closes: the company that looked like it was growing is a company that has been fifteen thousand euros short every month, growing.

## What to ask for

> "Show me this same chart with the axis starting at zero, and with the full year instead of just this window. Does the story still hold?"

The test itself, asked directly. It costs nothing, and it either confirms the chart or names the choice that was carrying it.

> "Show me costs on the same chart as revenue, not revenue alone."

Catches the aggregation problem specifically — growing revenue that's actually being outpaced by growing costs, hidden by a chart that only ever shows one side of the story.

> "Could someone unfamiliar with this business understand what this chart is claiming within about ten seconds, without you explaining it? If not, simplify it — fewer metrics, one clear comparison, or split it into more than one chart."

The complexity test. A chart needing a guided tour has failed at the only thing it was for, however correct every number on it is.
